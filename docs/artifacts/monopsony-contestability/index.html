Monopsony and Contestability

What happens when the power sits with the buyer, and why the threat of new entrants can discipline even a firm with a big market share.

The big idea

A monopsony is a market with a single (or dominant) buyer. Where a monopoly is a seller with power over the price consumers pay, a monopsonist is a buyer with power over the price it pays to workers or suppliers. A contestable market is one where firms are held in check by the threat of entry, whatever the number of firms already in it.

3.4.6 Monopsony

Characteristics and conditions

  • One dominant buyer and many sellers
  • Sellers (workers or suppliers) have few alternative buyers
  • Buyer can influence the price it pays: a price maker in the buying market
  • Often large employers in small areas, or big retailers buying from many small farmers or manufacturers
  • Examples: supermarkets buying from farmers, a single large employer in a remote town, the NHS buying nurses' labour

Why it pays less than the competitive price

To hire one more worker, a monopsonist must raise the wage not only for that worker but for all existing workers. So the marginal cost of labour (MCL) lies above the supply curve (average cost of labour). The firm hires where MCL = MRP, then pays only the wage read off the supply curve, which is lower than the competitive wage, and employs fewer workers.

3.4.7 Contestability

Characteristics of a contestable market (3.4.7a)

  • Low barriers to entry and exit
  • Low or no sunk costs
  • Entrants have access to the same technology and costs as incumbents
  • Good information about profit opportunities
  • Firms can enter and leave quickly: "hit-and-run" entry

Implications for firms (3.4.7b)

  • Incumbents must keep price close to average cost and avoid supernormal profit, or entrants will undercut them
  • Incentive to be productively efficient and avoid X-inefficiency
  • May use limit pricing or other deterrents (advertising, capacity)
  • Even a monopolist may behave like a competitive firm

Barriers to entry and exit (3.4.7c)

TypeExamples
LegalPatents, copyright, licences, regulation, government franchise
Structural / costEconomies of scale, high start-up costs, control of key resources or distribution, brand loyalty, network effects, switching costs
Strategic (behaviour of incumbents)Limit pricing, predatory pricing, heavy advertising, exclusive contracts, excess capacity as a threat
Exit barriersRedundancy costs, specialised assets with no resale value, long contracts

Sunk costs (3.4.7d)

Sunk costs are costs that cannot be recovered when a firm leaves a market, such as advertising, specialised equipment or staff training. The higher the sunk costs, the riskier entry is and the less contestable the market. Leasing a plane for a route has low sunk costs; building a new factory has high sunk costs.

Evaluation pointContestability depends on potential entry. A market with few firms can behave competitively if barriers are low; a market with many firms can be uncompetitive if barriers are high. Real markets sit on a spectrum.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by monopsony power.
Monopsony power exists when a single or dominant buyer purchases most of a market's output or labour. Because sellers have few alternative buyers, the monopsonist can influence the price it pays, and pay less than in a competitive market. For example, a large supermarket buying from many small farmers can push down the price it pays for milk. The farmers may have little choice but to accept, or lose their main route to market.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by a sunk cost and why it affects contestability.
A sunk cost is a cost that cannot be recovered when a firm leaves the market. Examples include specialist equipment with no resale value or money spent on advertising. High sunk costs mean a new entrant risks losing money if it fails, which raises the risk of entry and discourages it. The market is therefore less contestable, and incumbents are less constrained by the threat of entry and may earn supernormal profit for longer.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a monopsony employer is likely to affect wages and employment in a local labour market.
ReminderMCL above supply is the key step. Compare with a competitive market.

A monopsony employer is the main buyer of labour in the area, so it faces an upward-sloping labour supply curve. To hire an extra worker it must raise the wage for everyone, so the marginal cost of labour is above the wage. It maximises profit by hiring where MCL = MRP, which is at a lower level of employment than the competitive equilibrium. It then pays only the wage on the supply curve at that level of employment, below both the competitive wage and the MRP of the last worker hired. In a small town with one large factory, workers with few other local jobs may have to accept the lower wage. Wages and employment are therefore both likely to be lower than in a competitive market.

Q4 Assess 10 marks Assess the likely effects of a supermarket's monopsony power on its suppliers and its customers. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)

A supermarket with large market share can use its power as a dominant buyer to bargain down the prices it pays to suppliers. Suppliers such as farmers may earn lower profit, and some may be forced to cut costs, reduce quality or leave the industry, reducing supply in the long run. Customers could benefit from the lower input costs if the supermarket passes them on as lower prices, especially in a competitive retail market. Cheap milk or vegetables may be a result.

However, if suppliers leave or quality falls, customers could face less choice, poorer quality and, eventually, higher prices. The supermarket might also keep the cost savings as profit if there is little competition at retail level. Overall, the effect on suppliers is likely to be negative and the effect on customers depends on how competitive retailing is and how much of the saving is passed on. In the short run customers probably gain; the long-run effect is more uncertain.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks A market has only two airlines on a route, but a low-cost rival could easily start flying it. Discuss whether passengers are likely to be harmed by the lack of competition. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)

The market looks like a duopoly, but if barriers to entry and exit are low it is contestable. Aircraft can be leased, so sunk costs are low, and a rival could enter and exit quickly if fares rise (hit-and-run). The threat of entry may force the two incumbents to keep fares close to average cost and avoid supernormal profit, so passengers may not be harmed. The incumbents may also use limit pricing and offer good service to deter entry.

However, if airport slots are limited or the airlines own most of them, or if brand loyalty and frequent-flyer schemes lock in customers, barriers may be higher than they look. The incumbents could then charge higher fares and passengers could be harmed.

The conclusion depends on how real the entry threat is: slot availability, sunk costs and how quickly incumbents can react. If entry really is easy, passengers are likely to be protected; if slots are scarce, they are less so.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "A contestable market is as good for consumers as a perfectly competitive one." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanUse the definition of contestability and then compare outcomes: price, efficiency, choice, profit, innovation.
  1. Define contestable markets and perfect competition (price = AC, normal profit, productive efficiency).
  2. For the view: threat of entry forces normal profit and efficiency even with few firms, so prices near cost; allows economies of scale; incumbents avoid X-inefficiency.
  3. Against: contestable firms can still be allocatively inefficient (P above MC), may reduce investment for fear of sunk costs, limit pricing sacrifices some profit, less choice if products homogeneous.
  4. Real markets rarely fully contestable: sunk costs, brands and regulation matter. Strategic barriers (predatory pricing) can undermine contestability.
  5. Judgement: contestability can deliver outcomes close to competition if barriers and sunk costs are very low, otherwise not. Depends on the industry.

Top tips

  • Monopsony: say "single or dominant buyer" and name what is being bought (labour or supplies).
  • Labour diagram: MCL above supply, hire where MCL = MRP, pay the wage from the supply curve.
  • Minimum wage in monopsony can raise employment: a classic evaluation point.
  • Contestable markets: focus on potential entry, sunk costs and hit-and-run.
  • Name the barrier (legal, structural, strategic) and explain why it deters entry.
  • Evaluate with real-world barriers, how big the sunk costs are and whether the threat is credible.
