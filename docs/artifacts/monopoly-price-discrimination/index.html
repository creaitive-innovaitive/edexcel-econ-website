Monopoly and Price Discrimination

One dominant seller with the power to set price: how the profit-maximising monopolist behaves, why it is allocatively inefficient yet sometimes beneficial, how third-degree price discrimination works, and what makes a monopoly natural.

The big idea

A monopoly is a firm that has the market to itself or dominates it. With no close rivals and high barriers to entry, it is a price maker: it faces the whole downward-sloping market demand curve and can restrict output to raise price. It earns supernormal profit that entry cannot erode. Whether that is good or bad for society depends on what the firm does with its power.

Characteristics (3.4.5a)

What defines it

  • Pure monopoly: one seller. In practice the UK competition authorities treat a firm with about 25% or more of a market as having monopoly power.
  • High barriers to entry: legal (patents, licences), economies of scale, control of resources, brand strength, aggressive tactics.
  • Price maker, downward-sloping AR, MR below AR.
  • Supernormal profit can persist in the long run.

Profit-maximising equilibrium (3.4.5b, c)

  1. Find output where MC = MR.
  2. Go up to the AR curve to read the price.
  3. Read AC at that output: profit = (P − AC) × Q.

Price is above MC, so the monopolist produces less and charges more than a competitive market would.

Natural monopoly (3.4.5f)

A natural monopoly arises when economies of scale are so large that long-run average cost keeps falling over the whole range of market demand. One firm can supply the market at lower cost than several could, so competition would waste resources (duplicate pipes, rails or cables). Examples: water pipes, the electricity grid, railway track. The problem is that the firm can still exploit its power, so natural monopolies are usually regulated (see 3.6).

Third-degree price discrimination (3.4.5d)

Charging different prices for the same product to different groups of consumers, where the cost of supply is the same, to raise profit.

Necessary conditions

  • The firm must have market power (be a price maker).
  • It must be able to separate the groups (by age, time, location or identity).
  • The groups must have different price elasticities of demand.
  • Resale must be prevented (no arbitrage), otherwise the low-price group would sell to the high-price group.

How it works

Treat each group as a separate market. In each, produce where MC = MR. The group with less elastic demand pays the higher price. The firm earns more profit than with one price because it extracts more consumer surplus.

Examples: cinema tickets for students and adults, peak and off-peak rail fares, airline tickets bought early or late.

Benefits

  • Producers: higher profit; can fund investment
  • Consumers: some who would not buy at the single price can now buy (more output); profit may keep services running, e.g. off-peak trains

Costs

  • Consumers: those with inelastic demand pay more; lose consumer surplus; may feel unfair
  • Costs of separating markets and policing resale
Do not mix upPrice discrimination is not charging different prices because costs differ. It is charging different prices for the same product when costs are the same. Also, it is always third degree here: separate groups, one price each.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by a natural monopoly.
A natural monopoly occurs when long-run average cost falls over the whole range of market demand because of very large economies of scale. One firm can then supply the whole market at lower average cost than two or more firms could, because each extra firm would duplicate fixed costs. Water pipes or the electricity grid are examples: building several networks would be wasteful. Competition would therefore raise costs, though the monopolist could still charge high prices unless regulated.
Q2 Calculate 2 marks A monopolist sells 50 units at £30 each. Its average cost is £22. Calculate its supernormal profit.
Profit = (AR − AC) × Q. (30 − 22) × 50 = £400.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks A cinema charges lower prices for students than for adults. Analyse the conditions needed for this price discrimination to be profitable.
ReminderThree or four conditions, each with its reason.

The cinema must have some market power, so that it can set its own price rather than take it from the market. It must be able to identify and separate the groups, for example by requiring student ID, otherwise adults would claim the cheaper price. The groups must have different price elasticities of demand: students are likely to be more price elastic, so a lower price raises their quantity demanded by a bigger percentage and raises revenue, while adults, being less elastic, would pay more. The cinema must prevent resale, which is simple because tickets are used by the named person on the night. When these hold, setting MC = MR in each market lets the cinema raise total profit above what a single price would earn.

Q4 Assess 10 marks Assess whether a monopoly is always against the interests of consumers. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)

A monopolist maximises profit where MC = MR and charges a price above marginal cost. Compared with a competitive market, output is lower and price higher, so consumer surplus falls and there is a deadweight loss, which means allocative inefficiency. Without competitive pressure, the firm may also be X-inefficient, so costs and prices may be higher still.

However, a monopolist may benefit from large economies of scale, which could allow it to charge lower prices than several small firms. This is especially true for a natural monopoly such as a water network. Supernormal profit could also fund research and development, producing better products, and the threat of entry (contestability) or regulation may limit the firm's power. Overall, whether a monopoly harms consumers depends on how big the economies of scale are, how strongly the firm is regulated and whether the market is contestable. It is not always against consumers' interests.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Rail companies charge lower prices off-peak than at peak times. Discuss the likely effects of this pricing on consumers and the firm. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)

This is third-degree price discrimination: the same service sold to two groups at different prices. Commuters have inelastic demand because they must travel at peak times, so a higher price is likely to raise revenue; leisure travellers are more price elastic, so a lower off-peak price raises the number of journeys. The firm earns more profit than with a single price, which may allow it to run more services. Off-peak passengers, such as pensioners or day-trippers, may be able to travel who could not afford a single average price.

But commuters pay more and lose consumer surplus. They have few alternatives, so they cannot easily avoid the higher fares. The firm also faces costs of managing different fares and preventing peak travellers using off-peak tickets.

The effect on consumers overall depends on the balance between those who gain from lower fares and those who lose. Where the extra revenue funds more services and spreads demand away from congested peak trains, the net effect on society could be positive. If it mainly raises the firm's profit, the benefit is likely to be smaller.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Monopolies always reduce economic welfare." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanUse the diagram. Explain deadweight loss first. Then give the benefits, then conditions that decide the verdict.
  1. Define monopoly, show MC = MR, price from AR, supernormal profit, and the deadweight loss against perfect competition.
  2. Against: higher price, lower output, allocative and X-inefficiency, exploitation of suppliers and workers, barriers to entry, possible price discrimination.
  3. For: economies of scale, dynamic efficiency (R&D funded by profit), natural monopolies, international competitiveness, price discrimination that widens access.
  4. Counter-points: how far profit is actually reinvested; whether scale economies are passed on; regulation and contestability limit power; government failure.
  5. Judgement: "always" is too strong. Welfare depends on cost conditions, how contestable the market is, and how well it is regulated.

Top tips

  • Find MC = MR first, then go up to AR for the price. Many candidates read the price off MR by mistake.
  • Draw MR twice as steep as AR for a straight-line demand curve.
  • Shade profit as the rectangle (P − AC) × Q using AC at the profit-maximising output.
  • Deadweight loss is the triangle between the demand and MC curves, from monopoly output to competitive output.
  • Price discrimination: list the conditions, then use the elasticities: the less elastic group pays more.
  • Evaluate monopoly with economies of scale, dynamic efficiency, contestability and regulation. Do not just list costs.
  • Natural monopoly: falling LRAC over the whole market; mention regulation.
