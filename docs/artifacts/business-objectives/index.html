Business Objectives

Profit is not the only thing a firm can aim for. How profit, revenue and sales maximisation differ on a diagram, why managers and owners may want different things, and what satisficing means.

The big idea

The standard assumption is that firms maximise profit. In practice, owners, managers and other stakeholders may want something else, and each objective means a different price and output. Edexcel expects you to know four objectives, the diagram for the first three and the formula that identifies each. Spec 3.2.1.

The four objectives

ObjectiveWhat the firm aims forRuleCompared with profit maximisation
Profit maximisationThe largest gap between total revenue and total costMC = MRBaseline: lowest output of the three, highest price
Revenue maximisationThe highest total revenue (turnover)MR = 0More output, lower price, lower profit
Sales (output) maximisationThe most units sold, without making a lossAR = ACHighest output of the three, lowest price, only normal profit
SatisficingA satisfactory (not maximum) level of profit, enough to keep owners contentNo single ruleBelow maximum profit; managers pursue other goals

Why firms depart from profit maximisation

Revenue or sales maximisation

  • Principal-agent problem: managers' pay, status and job security often rise with the size of the firm, so they may prefer revenue or sales to profit.
  • Market share: a bigger share may deter rivals and give long-run power.
  • Economies of scale: more output may lower unit costs in the long run.
  • Survival or growth in a new or competitive market.

Satisficing

  • Owner-managers may be content with enough income and a quiet life.
  • Hard to know the exact profit-maximising output (limited information).
  • Managers balance several stakeholders: workers, customers, the community.
  • Fear of attracting competition or regulation if profits look excessive.

Firms can also pursue other goals: corporate social responsibility, environmental targets, or (for charities and public bodies) service targets. Objectives can change over time, for example from sales maximisation when a firm is new to profit maximisation once it is established.

Reading the diagram (3.2.1b)

  1. Draw AR (demand), MR (twice as steep), MC and AC. MC cuts AC at its lowest point.
  2. Profit max: go to MC = MR, then up to AR for price and across from AC for cost. Profit is the rectangle between them.
  3. Revenue max: go to where MR crosses the quantity axis (MR = 0). Output is higher, price lower.
  4. Sales max: go to where AR meets AC. Output is higher still, and profit is only normal.
Exam favouriteTotal revenue is largest where MR = 0, which is where demand is unit elastic. A revenue-maximising firm therefore always operates on the elastic or unit elastic part of its demand curve. Link this to 3.3.1.
Do not mix upRevenue maximisation is not sales maximisation. Revenue max is where MR = 0; sales max is where AR = AC (the firm still makes normal profit). Sales max output is higher than revenue max output in the standard diagram.

Descriptions are kept general and based on widely reported events. Check current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Amazon and growth before profit

For many years Amazon reported thin profits while it reinvested heavily in warehouses, delivery and cloud computing. Investors tolerated this because the firm was winning market share and growing sales quickly.

Exam use: a real case of prioritising revenue and market share over short-run profit. Evaluate: this works when future profit is expected and investors are patient.

Uber and subsidised growth

Ride-hailing firms spent heavily on driver incentives and discounted fares to build networks of riders and drivers, with large losses reported for years before the business moved towards profit.

Exam use: sales maximisation and entry strategy. Discuss the link with network effects and barriers to entry.

Executive pay and share options

Many large companies link senior pay to profit or share price through bonuses and share options, to reduce the gap between managers and shareholders.

Exam use: a solution to the principal-agent problem. Evaluate: pay tied to short-term share price can encourage managers to chase quick results.

Family businesses and satisficing

The owners of many small shops, farms and garages choose not to expand. They earn enough to meet their needs and prefer control, time and independence to extra profit.

Exam use: satisficing in practice, and a reason firms remain small (see 3.1).

Social enterprises and charities

Charities and social enterprises aim to cover costs and use any surplus for a social purpose. Their objective is service delivery or impact rather than profit.

Exam use: not-for-profit objectives as an alternative to profit maximisation.

Q1 Calculate 4 marks A firm moves from profit maximisation (price £28, output 6, average cost £13.33) to revenue maximisation (price £20, output 10, average cost £16). Calculate the change in total supernormal profit.
ReminderProfit = (AR − AC) × Q for each case. Then take the difference.
Profit = (AR − AC) × Q. Profit maximisation: (28 − 13.33) × 6 ≈ £88. Revenue maximisation: (20 − 16) × 10 = £40. Change = 40 − 88 = −£48 (profit falls by about £48).
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain why managers might choose revenue maximisation rather than profit maximisation.
Managers (agents) may have different aims from shareholders (principals), who usually want profit. Managers' pay, status and job security often rise with the size of the firm, so they may choose to maximise revenue, which means producing more and charging less than at profit maximisation. For example, the manager of a supermarket chain paid on turnover may open unprofitable stores. Shareholders may find it hard to monitor this because managers know more about the firm (asymmetric information).
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a profit-maximising firm's price and output differ from those of a sales-maximising firm. Use a diagram.
ReminderMC = MR versus AR = AC. Say which output is bigger and which price is lower.

A profit maximiser produces where MC = MR, then reads its price from AR. A sales maximiser produces as much as possible without making a loss, where AR = AC.

On the diagram, AR = AC is to the right of MC = MR, so output is higher. Because demand slopes down, the higher output must be sold at a lower price, so price is lower under sales maximisation. At AR = AC the firm earns only normal profit, so supernormal profit is lower than at profit maximisation.

Q4 Assess 10 marks Assess whether profit maximisation is likely to be the main objective of a family-run restaurant. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)

Profit maximisation means producing where MC = MR. In a small owner-managed restaurant there is little separation of ownership and control, so the principal-agent problem is small and the owner can pursue profit directly. Competition from other restaurants may also force the owner to keep costs low and prices competitive in order to survive.

However, family owners may prefer satisficing: enough income, with time for family and control over quality. They may keep prices below the profit-maximising level to build loyalty, or use ingredients of a higher cost for pride in the food.

It depends on the owner's priorities, the level of competition and whether the firm has debt to repay. Without good information the owner may also not know the exact profit-maximising output. Overall, the aim is likely to be a satisfactory profit rather than the maximum.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss the likely effects on consumers of a firm changing its objective from profit maximisation to sales maximisation. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)

Sales maximisation means producing the highest output possible without making a loss, where AR = AC. The firm produces more and, because demand slopes down, charges a lower price, so consumers pay less and more is available. Consumer surplus is likely to rise. The firm moves closer to allocative efficiency (price nearer to MC). A supermarket cutting prices to win share is an example.

However, with only normal profit the firm has little to invest in research or quality, so dynamic efficiency may suffer. If it cuts costs to keep prices low, quality may fall.

The effect depends on how long sales maximisation lasts and whether the firm uses its larger scale to cut costs. If it drives out rivals and later raises prices, the long-run result could be worse for consumers. In the short run, consumers are likely to gain.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Revenue maximisation is a more realistic business objective than profit maximisation." To what extent do you agree? (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanDefine each objective with a diagram. Reasons for revenue max, reasons for profit max, then conditions.
  1. Define profit and revenue maximisation (MC = MR; MR = 0) and show them on a diagram, with the other objectives.
  2. For revenue max: principal-agent problem, manager pay linked to size, market share, economies of scale, survival and growth, positive link to PED (operates on elastic part).
  3. For profit max: owner-managed firms, shareholder pressure, takeover threat, cost of lower profit (less investment), limited access to finance.
  4. Other objectives: satisficing, sales max, CSR. Objectives differ by size, ownership and market.
  5. Judgement: "more realistic" depends on ownership and competition; use an industry. Most large listed firms trade off profit and growth.

Top tips

  • Memorise the three rules: profit MC = MR; revenue MR = 0; sales AR = AC.
  • Draw all four curves. MC must cut AC at its lowest point.
  • Order of output: profit max < revenue max < sales max. Prices go the other way.
  • Profit at each point is (AR − AC) × Q. Sales max has only normal profit.
  • Explain the reason for a different objective: principal-agent, market share, survival, owner preferences.
  • Satisficing has no single formula: describe it in words.
  • Evaluate with ownership, size, competition and time horizon.
