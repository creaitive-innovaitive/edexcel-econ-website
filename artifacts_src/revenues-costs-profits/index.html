Revenues, Costs and Profits

The toolkit behind every business diagram: total, average and marginal revenue, the cost curves and why they are U-shaped, economies and diseconomies of scale, and when a firm earns profit, breaks even or shuts down.

The big idea

Every firm decision comes down to comparing the extra revenue from selling more with the extra cost of making it. This chapter gives you the definitions, formulae and curves you need for that comparison, and the diagrams for normal profit, supernormal profit, loss and shut-down. Spec 3.3.1 to 3.3.4.

3.3.1 Revenue

TR = P × Q AR = TR ÷ Q = P MR = change in TR ÷ change in Q

AR is the same as price, so the AR curve is the firm's demand curve. When a firm must cut price to sell more, the lower price applies to every unit, so MR is below AR and falls twice as fast on a straight-line demand curve. A price taker (perfect competition) has a horizontal demand curve: AR = MR = P.

3.3.2 Costs

TC = TFC + TVC AC = TC ÷ Q AVC = TVC ÷ Q AFC = TFC ÷ Q MC = change in TC ÷ change in Q
  • Short run: at least one factor (usually capital) is fixed. Long run: all factors can be varied.
  • Fixed costs (rent, insurance, loan repayments) do not change with output. Variable costs (materials, hourly wages, energy used in production) do.
  • AC = AVC + AFC. AFC falls continually as output rises (fixed costs are spread over more units).

Why the cost curves are U-shaped: diminishing marginal productivity

With one factor fixed (say one factory), adding workers first raises output by more and more as they specialise: marginal product rises and MC falls. Eventually workers get in each other's way, and each extra worker adds less than the last. Marginal product falls, so MC rises. That gives MC its U (or tick) shape, and AVC and AC follow.

Exam favouriteMC cuts AVC and AC at their lowest points. When MC is below an average it pulls the average down; when above, it drags it up. If you draw MC through any other point, you lose the mark.

Short-run and long-run average costs

Each short-run average cost curve (SRAC) belongs to one fixed scale of capital. In the long run the firm can choose any scale, so the long-run average cost curve (LRAC) is the lower envelope of all the SRAC curves: the lowest cost of producing each output when everything can be varied.

3.3.3 Economies and diseconomies of scale

Economies of scale are falls in LRAC as output rises (the downward-sloping part of LRAC). Diseconomies of scale push LRAC back up.

Internal economies

  • Technical: large machinery, production lines, the increased-dimensions effect
  • Purchasing: bulk-buy discounts
  • Managerial: specialist managers become affordable
  • Financial: larger firms borrow at lower interest rates
  • Marketing: advertising spread over more units
  • Risk-bearing: diversified products and markets

External economies and diseconomies

External economies come from the size of the whole industry: supplier clusters, a pool of skilled labour, shared infrastructure, specialist colleges (Silicon Valley, the City of London).

Internal diseconomies: poor communication, slow decisions, weaker motivation and control in very large firms. External diseconomies: congestion and rising rents and wages as many firms compete for the same land and workers.

Minimum efficient scale (MES) is the lowest output at which a firm reaches the bottom of its LRAC. If MES is a large share of market demand, only a few firms can be efficient, which helps explain natural monopoly and oligopoly.

3.3.4 Profit

Profit = TR − TC Profit per unit = AR − AC
  • Profit-maximising condition: MC = MR.
  • Normal profit: the minimum reward needed to keep the entrepreneur in the industry, an opportunity cost included in AC. It occurs where AR = AC.
  • Supernormal profit: above normal, where AR > AC. In competitive markets it attracts new entrants.
  • Loss: AR < AC.

Shut-down points

Short run: fixed costs must be paid whether the firm produces or not. So the firm keeps producing while AR ≥ AVC, because it covers all variable costs and contributes towards fixed costs. It shuts down if AR < AVC. Long run: all costs are variable, so the firm leaves the industry if AR < AC.

6 · Multiple choice

Descriptions are kept general and based on widely reported events. Check current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Ryanair and bulk buying

Ryanair has operated a very large fleet of one aircraft type and has placed huge orders with the manufacturer, which is reported to give it substantial discounts. Using one type also cuts training and maintenance costs.

Exam use: purchasing and technical economies of scale, then the chain: lower unit cost → lower LRAC → lower fares or higher margin.

Industry clusters: Silicon Valley and the City of London

Technology firms in California and financial firms in London benefit from a deep pool of skilled workers, specialist suppliers and universities nearby. Each firm's costs are lower because of the size of the industry in the area.

Exam use: external economies of scale. Evaluate with external diseconomies such as high rents and wages.

Big public IT projects

Large government IT programmes, such as the NHS National Programme for IT which was dismantled in 2011, have often run over budget and over time. Critics link this to the difficulty of coordinating very large organisations.

Exam use: a link to diseconomies of scale: communication and coordination problems, and loss of control.

Seaside cafes in winter

Many seasonal businesses close for part of the year. If takings in winter do not cover the variable costs of opening (staff, ingredients, heating), it is better to shut for the season even though fixed costs such as rent still have to be paid.

Exam use: the short-run shut-down rule: produce only while AR ≥ AVC. Fixed costs are sunk in the short run.

Fertiliser plants and the 2022 gas price spike

When wholesale gas prices jumped in 2022, a major fertiliser producer paused ammonia production at two UK sites, because gas is a main variable cost and the price it could charge did not cover it.

Exam use: a rise in variable costs shifts MC and AVC up, and the firm shuts down in the short run if AR falls below AVC. Links to cost and revenue diagram questions.

Airline fare cuts

Low-cost airlines have often cut fares to fill seats. Where demand is elastic, a fall in price can raise total revenue, and since an empty seat has a very low marginal cost, extra passengers are worth having.

Exam use: price elasticity of demand and revenue: elastic demand means MR > 0, so cutting price raises TR.

Q1 Calculate 2 marks A firm's total revenue rises from £600 to £660 when output rises from 40 to 44 units. Calculate marginal revenue.
MR = change in total revenue ÷ change in quantity. (660 − 600) ÷ (44 − 40) = 60 ÷ 4 = £15.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain why marginal cost cuts average cost at its lowest point.
Marginal cost is the cost of producing one more unit and average cost is total cost per unit. When MC is below AC, producing one more unit costs less than the current average, so AC falls. When MC is above AC, the extra unit costs more than the average, so AC rises. AC therefore stops falling and starts rising where MC = AC, which is the lowest point of the curve.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how economies of scale could lower a firm's long-run average costs.
ReminderPick two economies that fit the firm, and chain each to LRAC.

Economies of scale are falls in long-run average cost as output rises. Purchasing economies: a large firm can buy materials in bulk and negotiate discounts, so the cost per unit of inputs is lower, which lowers LRAC. A supermarket chain buying in large volumes could get better prices from suppliers.

Technical economies: large machines and production lines are only worth buying at high output, but then each unit costs less to make, spreading the high cost over many units. Managerial economies could also arise as specialist managers become affordable, raising efficiency. As a result, the firm could cut prices or raise profit per unit.

Q4 Assess 10 marks A seaside cafe is earning less than its variable costs in winter but has fixed costs it must pay all year. Assess whether it should close for the winter. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)

In the short run a firm should produce if AR is at least equal to average variable cost, because it then covers its variable costs and contributes towards fixed costs. If winter takings are below variable costs, every day open adds to the loss beyond the fixed costs, which have to be paid whether it opens or not. Closing saves ingredients, staff pay and heating, so the cafe would lose only its fixed costs.

However, closing could lose regular customers or staff who may not return, and some costs may be hard to avoid. If takings are close to variable costs, or there are likely to be future benefits from staying open, it could be worth staying open. Overall, the cafe should close if AR is clearly below AVC and the loss of goodwill is small. In the long run it should only stay in business if it can cover all costs, including fixed costs.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Energy prices have risen sharply. Discuss the likely impact of this on a bakery's profits. Use a cost and revenue diagram. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)
ReminderA rise in a variable cost shifts MC and AC (and AVC) up. Draw it, then show profit falling.

Energy is a variable cost for a bakery, so a rise in its price raises the cost of each extra loaf: MC and AC shift up. On the diagram, with demand (AR and MR) unchanged, the new MC = MR point is at a lower output. The gap between AR and AC narrows, so profit falls, and may turn to a loss. An oven-heavy bakery faces larger cost increases than a firm using little energy.

If the bakery raises price, quantity demanded falls, so revenue may fall too, especially if demand is elastic. It may absorb the cost rise instead, which cuts profit further.

The effect depends on how much of total cost is energy, how elastic demand is, whether rivals face the same increase (so prices rise across the market), and whether the rise is temporary. A bakery with spare capacity or the ability to pass on costs may protect profit.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Large firms always benefit from economies of scale." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanUse LRAC and MES. Give economies, then diseconomies and conditions.
  1. Define economies of scale, LRAC, MES and show the LRAC curve.
  2. Internal economies: technical, purchasing, managerial, financial, marketing, risk-bearing, each chained to a fall in LRAC and a benefit (lower prices, higher profit, competitiveness).
  3. External economies for firms in a cluster.
  4. Diseconomies: coordination, motivation and control problems; external congestion and rising rents.
  5. Conditions: the size of MES relative to the market, the type of industry, how well the firm is managed, the time period.
  6. Judgement: "always" is too strong. Economies are likely up to MES, but beyond it diseconomies may outweigh them.

Top tips

  • Write the formula first and show working on every calculation.
  • MR below AR. MR is twice as steep for a straight-line demand curve.
  • MC cuts AVC and AC at their minimum points. Mark them.
  • AC = AVC + AFC. The gap between AC and AVC is AFC.
  • Explain the U shape with diminishing marginal productivity.
  • Pick the economy that fits the business and chain it to lower LRAC.
  • Shut-down: short run AR < AVC; long run AR < AC.
  • Evaluate with MES, diseconomies, time period and size of the cost change.
