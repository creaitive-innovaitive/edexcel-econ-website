The Labour Market

Why firms demand labour, what decides who supplies it, how wages are set in competitive and non-competitive markets, and what governments do when the market falls short.

The big idea

The labour market is a market like any other, with a twist: the "price" is the wage, the buyers are firms, and the demand for labour is derived demand. Firms hire workers because of what they help produce and sell. Where wages are set by competition, the market clears. Where power or rules interfere (monopsony, unions, minimum wages), outcomes change.

3.5.1 Demand for labour

Derived demand

Firms demand labour not for its own sake but to produce goods and services. If demand for the product rises, so does demand for the workers who make it.

Marginal revenue product

The extra revenue from employing one more worker:

MRP = MPP × MR (MR = price for a price taker)

The MRP curve is the firm's demand curve for labour. A profit-maximiser hires up to where MRP = the cost of the marginal worker. Diminishing marginal productivity makes MRP slope down.

What shifts the demand for labour

  • Demand for the product (derived demand): a rise shifts labour demand right.
  • Productivity of labour (MPP): training, better technology and management raise MRP.
  • Price of substitutes: if capital (machines) becomes cheaper, firms may substitute it for labour.
  • Non-wage labour costs such as employer taxes and regulation.
  • Price of the product: higher price raises MRP.

A change in the wage rate itself causes a movement along the demand curve, not a shift.

Elasticity of demand for labour (3.5.3d)

Demand for labour is less elastic when:

  • labour is a small proportion of total costs
  • it is hard to substitute capital for labour (skilled or creative work)
  • the product's demand is inelastic (firms can pass on wage rises)
  • the time period is short (firms cannot change production methods quickly)

3.5.2 Supply of labour to an occupation

  • Wage rate relative to other jobs: movement along the curve.
  • Non-wage benefits: pensions, holidays, hours, flexibility, job satisfaction, working conditions.
  • Qualifications and training needed: long training time restricts supply.
  • Population, age structure, participation rates and migration.
  • Barriers: trade unions, professional bodies and licensing can restrict entry.
  • Tax and benefits: they affect the incentive to work.

Elasticity of labour supply

Supply is less elastic where the job needs long training or specialist skills, where workers are immobile, and in the short run. It is more elastic where little training is needed and many unemployed workers are available.

Market failure: labour immobility

Geographical immobility

Workers cannot or will not move to where jobs are. Causes: high housing costs in job-rich areas, family and community ties, costs of moving, lack of information, uncertainty about a new area. Result: unemployment in some regions and shortages in others.

Occupational immobility

Workers cannot easily switch between jobs. Causes: skills mismatch, lack of qualifications, licensing and professional barriers, age, lack of retraining. Result: structural unemployment when industries decline.

3.5.3 Wage determination

Competitive labour market

Wages settle where labour demand = labour supply. A rise in demand for the product shifts labour demand right: wage and employment rise. Higher labour supply (for example from migration) shifts supply right: wage falls, employment rises. Wage differentials between occupations reflect differences in skill and MRP, training time, working conditions and bargaining power.

Current labour market issuesBe ready to use up-to-date examples: shortages in sectors such as health and construction, the gig economy and zero-hours contracts, automation and artificial intelligence, an ageing population, migration, and gender and regional pay gaps.

Trade unions

Unions bargain collectively. They may raise wages above the competitive level, reduce employment among members if labour demand is elastic, or counter monopsony power so that wages and employment both rise.

5 · Multiple choice

Descriptions are kept general and based on widely reported events. Check current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

HGV driver shortages

In 2021 the UK reported a shortage of tens of thousands of heavy-goods-vehicle drivers, with some firms offering bonuses and higher pay.

Exam use: Labour demand outpacing supply: a rise in wage and use of non-wage incentives. Link to occupational immobility (training and licensing take time).

The National Living Wage

The UK introduced a national minimum wage in 1999, advised by the Low Pay Commission. The adult rate has risen far faster than inflation in recent years (about £11.44 an hour in April 2024 and £12.21 from April 2025 for those aged 21 and over).

Exam use: Minimum wage evidence: studies have generally found small job losses, which supports monopsony and productivity arguments. Quote a current rate only if you are sure of it.

House prices and regional gaps

Average house prices in London are typically several times those in the North East of England. High housing costs make it hard for workers in lower-cost regions to move to better-paid jobs.

Exam use: Geographical immobility: cause and effect, and policy options such as affordable housing and relocation help.

Automation and self-service tills

Supermarkets have installed self-service tills, reducing the number of cashiers needed. Firms may use fewer workers where capital becomes cheaper or more productive, while demand grows for technicians and delivery staff.

Exam use: Substitution of capital for labour and its effect on labour demand. Link to occupational immobility and retraining.

NHS strikes and public sector pay

Nurses and junior doctors in England took strike action in 2022 to 2024 over pay and conditions. Public sector pay is set with the help of pay review bodies, and wage rises are limited by government budgets.

Exam use: Public sector wage setting and its limits: staff shortages, recruitment and retention problems, and the cost to taxpayers.

Gender pay gap reporting

Since 2017, UK employers with 250 or more staff have had to publish their gender pay gaps. Reasons given include occupational choices, career breaks and discrimination.

Exam use: Wage differentials and labour market imperfections: use it for evaluation questions on why pay differs between workers.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by the demand for labour being a derived demand.
Derived demand means that labour is demanded not for its own sake but because of the goods and services workers help to produce. If demand for the final product rises, firms need more output and so need more workers, so the demand for labour shifts right. For example, when demand for electric cars rises, car manufacturers are likely to demand more engineers and assembly workers. If demand for the product falls, demand for labour is likely to fall too.
Q2 Calculate 2 marks A firm's wage bill rises from £500,000 to £550,000 while employment falls from 100 to 95 workers. Calculate the percentage change in the average wage.
Average wage before: 500,000 ÷ 100 = £5,000. After: 550,000 ÷ 95 ≈ £5,789. Percentage change = (5,789 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 ≈ 15.8%.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse why the demand for labour might be less elastic in some occupations than in others.
ReminderGive two or three factors, each with a chain, and contrast two occupations.

Demand for labour is less elastic when labour is a small proportion of total costs. A wage rise then increases total costs only slightly, so the firm is likely to reduce employment by a small percentage. For example, a pilot is a small share of an airline's total cost per flight.

It is also less elastic when it is difficult to substitute capital for labour: where skills or personal service are essential, firms cannot easily replace workers with machines, so a wage rise is unlikely to cut employment much. Surgeons are hard to replace with technology, whereas supermarket cashiers could be replaced by self-service tills, making demand for them more elastic. Demand is also less elastic when the product's demand is inelastic, since higher wage costs can be passed on in prices.

Q4 Assess 10 marks Assess the effectiveness of two policies to reduce geographical immobility of labour. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)

Geographical immobility means workers cannot or will not move to where jobs are, often because of high housing costs and family ties. Building more affordable housing in job-rich areas could reduce the cost of moving, making relocation more attractive and reducing regional unemployment and shortages. Relocation grants or help with moving costs could cover the up-front cost of a move, encouraging unemployed workers to take jobs elsewhere. Government help for workers moving from declining coal regions to growing cities would be an example.

However, housing policy is slow and expensive, and new homes take years to build, so the effect may only come in the long run. Grants may have limited effect if the main obstacles are family ties or unfamiliarity rather than money, and they cost the government money. Overall, relocation grants may work faster but have a small effect; housing supply is more effective but slower. The best approach is likely to combine both with better information and transport.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss whether a rise in the national minimum wage is likely to benefit low-paid workers. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)

A minimum wage is a price floor set above the market wage for low-skilled labour. Workers who keep their jobs gain higher income, which could reduce poverty, raise spending and improve incentives to work. Higher pay could also raise productivity and reduce staff turnover, offsetting some of the cost to firms. Care workers and retail assistants are examples of low-paid groups.

In a competitive labour market, a wage above equilibrium reduces the quantity of labour demanded and increases the quantity supplied, causing unemployment. Firms may cut hours or jobs, raise prices, or replace workers with machines. If the employer has monopsony power, the minimum wage could increase both wages and employment.

The overall effect depends on how large the rise is relative to the existing wage, how elastic labour demand is in the sector, whether employers have monopsony power and the state of the economy. A moderate rise is more likely to benefit workers than a very large one. Those who lose jobs are the low-paid workers the policy was designed to help.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Differences in wages between occupations are mainly explained by differences in productivity." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanProductivity (MRP) is the starting point. Then supply factors, power and discrimination.
  1. Wages in competitive markets are determined by demand (MRP) and supply. Define MRP and derived demand.
  2. For: higher MRP (skills, productivity, product price) raises labour demand and wages: surgeons, specialist engineers.
  3. Supply: long training, qualifications, risk and unpleasant conditions limit supply and raise wages; professional bodies and unions restrict entry.
  4. Non-competitive factors: monopsony employers, trade unions, minimum wage, public-sector pay setting, discrimination and gender pay gaps.
  5. MRP is hard to measure in the public sector, and in teams. Productivity differences matter most in competitive private markets; elsewhere power and policy matter.
  6. Judgement: productivity is important but only part of the explanation. It depends on the occupation and on how competitive the market is.

Top tips

  • Say "derived demand" and link it to the product market.
  • Movement or shift? A wage change moves along the curves. Everything else shifts them.
  • MRP = MPP × MR. The demand curve for labour is the MRP curve.
  • Minimum wage diagram: mark the floor above equilibrium, then read labour demanded and supplied. The gap is unemployment.
  • Evaluate the minimum wage with elasticity of demand, monopsony power, size of rise and productivity effects.
  • Immobility: give a cause and a result. Geographical is about where, occupational is about skills.
  • Use current examples (shortages, gig economy, automation) where relevant.
