Is the Rail Network a Natural Monopoly?

A worked 12-mark data response (Edexcel 6d): how the marks are split, how to use Extract A, paragraph 3, the diagram that carries the argument, and a colour-coded model answer.

The question

"With reference to Extract A, paragraph 3, discuss whether the rail network can be considered to be a natural monopoly." 12 marks. Discuss means two sides and a judgement. The extract is part of the answer, not background.

Using Extract A, paragraph 3

"2 500 stations and 32 000 km of tracks"

The scale of the fixed costs. Link it to LRAC falling as these costs are spread over more passengers.

"renationalised in 2001"

Competition was promised at privatisation (1993, from your own knowledge) but the network came back into state hands.

"the state Network Rail monopoly should be removed so that companies can bid to build new rail lines"

Evidence for the case against: government sees part of the network as open to competition.

"a supporter of private sector involvement"

Be critical of the evidence. This is a viewpoint from someone with a stake in the argument, so it does not prove competition would cut costs. The extract is headed "The case for nationalisation", so it leans the other way.

The case for and the case against

Natural monopoly

  • Huge fixed costs of track and stations; one more passenger or train adds little cost, so LRAC keeps falling.
  • Two or more networks mean wasteful duplication, higher average cost and higher fares.
  • Only one network can fully exploit technical, commercial and managerial economies of scale.
  • Allocative efficiency (P = MC) lies near the far end of demand, so large subsidies could be needed to fund off-peak services and cover losses.

Not a natural monopoly

  • Government support for competition: companies bidding to build new lines.
  • Train operating companies already compete to run trains on the lines.
  • Lines and stations could be leased to competing firms.
  • Possible diseconomies of scale: managerial slack, closure of lines.
  • Technology may allow competing lines.

Model answer

Answers are colour-coded to the mark scheme. This is an original practice answer, not past-paper wording.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Discuss 12 marks With reference to Extract A, paragraph 3, discuss whether the rail network can be considered to be a natural monopoly. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)

A natural monopoly exists when one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms could, because long run average cost keeps falling over the range of market demand.

Extract A describes a network of 2,500 stations and 32,000 km of track. Laying track and building stations are enormous fixed costs that do not rise when one more passenger boards. As output grows, these costs are spread over more passengers, so LRAC is likely to keep falling. On the diagram, demand cuts LRAC while it is still falling, so one firm serving the whole market could have a lower average cost than two firms each serving half. That firm alone can fully exploit technical, commercial and managerial economies of scale.

Duplicating the track could double the fixed costs, so each firm's average cost would be higher and the extra cost may be passed on as higher fares. This fits the extract: competition was promised at privatisation in 1993, yet the network was renationalised in 2001. Allocative efficiency, where P = MC, lies near the end of the demand curve, so efficient pricing is likely to leave losses on off-peak services and could need large subsidies.

However, the transport secretary quoted in the extract supports private sector involvement and argues that companies should bid to build new lines. This is a viewpoint from someone with a stake in it, so it does not prove competition would lower costs, but it shows government sees part of the network as contestable. Train operating companies already compete to run trains on the same track, so only the infrastructure may have the cost structure of a natural monopoly. Lines and stations could also be leased to competing firms, giving rivalry without duplicate track. A single network may also suffer diseconomies of scale, such as managerial slack or closure of unprofitable lines, which could push LRAC up.

Overall, the track and stations are best seen as a natural monopoly because the fixed costs are so large that duplication is likely to raise average cost and fares. Train services are not, so the answer depends on which part is meant, and leasing or bidding could add competition without losing the scale economies.

Top tips

  • Define natural monopoly, not monopoly. The idea is the cost structure: LRAC still falling at the quantity demanded.
  • Cost, not ownership. Nationalised and natural monopoly are different ideas. A natural monopoly can be privately owned.
  • Chain, do not list. "High fixed costs, economies of scale, subsidies" is three labels. Link each to a consequence.
  • Use paragraph 3 twice: the figures and 2001 for the case for, the secretary's view for the case against.
  • Question the evidence. The secretary supports private involvement and the extract is headed "The case for nationalisation".
  • Split the network. Track and stations fit the definition; train operators do not. This is your strongest evaluation point.
  • Finish with a judgement that says what your view depends on.
