Oligopoly

A few big firms who watch each other: how to measure concentration, why firms collude (and why it breaks down), game theory, and the many ways oligopolists compete.

The big idea

In an oligopoly a few firms dominate. Because each firm's decision on price or output affects its rivals, they are interdependent: before acting, each firm has to guess how the others will react. That uncertainty explains collusion, price wars, branding and game theory.

Characteristics (3.4.4a)

High barriers to entry and exit

Economies of scale, brand loyalty, high start-up costs, patents, control of distribution. Few new firms can join.

High concentration ratio

A small number of firms hold most of the market share.

Interdependence

A firm's best strategy depends on what rivals do. Firms try to predict and respond to each other's moves.

Product differentiation

Products are usually differentiated (cars, smartphones, supermarkets) though some are similar (petrol, steel).

Concentration ratios (3.4.4b)

The n-firm concentration ratio is the combined market share of the largest n firms.

CR(n) = share₁ + share₂ + … + shareₙ (the n largest firms)

Example: shares of 30%, 25%, 15% and 10% for the four largest firms give CR4 = 80%. A high ratio suggests an oligopoly and may mean the firms can behave similarly or collude. A CR4 of around 60% or more is often treated as high, but there is no fixed cut-off.

Evaluation pointA concentration ratio ignores how contestable the market is, whether firms are competing fiercely, the size of imports and the way the market is defined. A high CR does not prove the firms are exploiting consumers.

Collusion and its limits (3.4.4c, d)

Why firms may collude

  • Joint profit maximisation: act like a monopolist
  • Reduces uncertainty about rivals' reactions
  • Avoids costly price wars
  • Barriers keep new entrants out

Why collusion is hard or breaks down

  • Illegal in the UK and EU: heavy fines
  • Each firm has an incentive to cheat by undercutting
  • More firms make agreement harder
  • Firms have different costs and goals
  • New entrants or imports erode the agreement
  • Falling demand tempts firms to cut price
TypeWhat it isExample
Overt (formal) collusionAn open agreement on price or output; a cartel if several firms agreeOPEC (an international cartel of oil-producing countries)
Tacit collusionNo explicit agreement; firms follow a pattern, e.g. price leadership: one dominant firm changes price and the others followSupermarkets or petrol stations matching each other's prices

Game theory: the prisoner's dilemma (3.4.4e)

Game theory models strategic decisions where the outcome depends on what others choose. In a simple two-firm, two-outcome model each firm picks comply (keep to the agreed output) or cheat (produce more). A payoff matrix shows the profit each firm gets from every combination.

  • If both comply they share monopoly profit.
  • If one cheats while the other complies, the cheat gains and the complier loses.
  • If both cheat, price falls and both earn less than if both complied.

Whatever the rival does, cheating gives a higher payoff, so cheating is the dominant strategy. Both cheat and end up worse off. That is why cartels are unstable. Try it in the Explore tab.

Competition between oligopolists (3.4.4f, g)

Price competition

  • Price war: rivals repeatedly cut prices; profits fall for all and weaker firms may leave.
  • Predatory pricing: pricing below cost to force a rival out, then raising price. Illegal if proved.
  • Limit pricing: setting price just low enough (above cost) to make entry unprofitable, sacrificing some profit to deter new firms.

Non-price competition

  • Branding and advertising
  • Product development and innovation
  • Quality and after-sales service
  • Loyalty cards and discounts for regulars
  • Convenience and delivery

Firms prefer non-price competition because price cuts are easily matched and start price wars. It can raise costs and still leave consumers with real choice.

Descriptions are kept general and based on widely reported events. Check current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

UK supermarkets

A handful of large grocers have held roughly two-thirds of the UK grocery market, with discounters such as Aldi and Lidl growing their share. Rivals frequently match each other's prices on staples and run price-match promotions.

Exam use: Concentration ratio, interdependence and non-collusive price matching. The rise of the discounters shows how entry can erode an oligopoly.

OPEC and oil quotas

OPEC members, joined by other producers in OPEC+, agree output targets to support the oil price. Members have often produced above their quotas, and the rise of US shale oil has weakened the group's influence.

Exam use: A cartel in practice: the gains from collusion, the incentive to cheat and why such agreements are unstable.

The European truck cartel

Several European truck makers were found to have coordinated prices over many years, including passing on the costs of meeting emissions rules. The European Commission imposed fines of billions of euros in 2016 and 2017.

Exam use: Overt collusion, illegal in the UK and EU, and why firms collude (joint profit, shared cost shocks).

Apple and Samsung

Smartphone makers compete heavily on branding, design, camera quality and software ecosystems rather than mainly on price. Both firms have spent large sums on product development and advertising.

Exam use: Non-price competition in an oligopoly, differentiation and barriers to entry. Evaluate: R&D can be a benefit (dynamic efficiency) and a cost passed on in prices.

Q1 Calculate 2 marks The four largest firms in a market have shares of 28%, 22%, 15% and 9%. Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio.
CR4 = sum of the four largest shares. 28 + 22 + 15 + 9 = 74%.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain one reason why firms in an oligopoly may choose to collude.
ReminderOne reason, developed. "Higher profit" alone is not enough: show how.
Firms may collude to maximise joint profit by acting like a monopolist. By agreeing to restrict output or fix a higher price, they move away from competing on price, so industry output falls and price rises. For example, two airlines on the same route could agree to keep fares above the competitive level. Because the market has high barriers to entry, new firms are unlikely to undercut them, so the supernormal profit could be sustained.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse why cartels tend to be unstable. Use game theory in your answer.
ReminderDescribe the dilemma in words: what each firm gains from cheating, what happens if both cheat, and why they cannot trust each other.

A cartel is an agreement between firms to restrict output and raise price. Once price is high, each firm could raise its own profit by secretly producing more or undercutting, because it earns the high price on extra units while rivals bear the fall in price. In a prisoner's dilemma payoff matrix, cheating gives a higher profit whatever the other firm does, so cheating is a dominant strategy.

If both cheat, market output rises and price falls, so both earn less than if they had kept to the agreement. Because each firm fears the other will cheat first, the agreement is likely to collapse. OPEC members have often exceeded their agreed quotas, for example.

Q4 Assess 10 marks Assess whether a price war between supermarkets is likely to benefit consumers. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)
ReminderBenefits in the short run, risks in the long run. Remember suppliers and quality.

A price war occurs when rival firms repeatedly cut prices to win market share. In the short run consumers are likely to benefit from lower prices, which raises their real income and consumer surplus, particularly for essentials like food. Firms may also compete on quality and promotions to keep customers. Supermarkets that match prices on staples could lower household food bills.

However, falling margins may mean supermarkets squeeze suppliers, who may cut quality, wages or leave the market, and smaller rivals could be forced to exit, reducing choice in the long run. Once weaker firms have gone, remaining firms might raise prices again. Overall, price wars tend to benefit consumers in the short run, but the longer-term outcome depends on how long the war lasts, whether firms are driven out and whether prices recover.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Smartphone manufacturers rely heavily on branding and product development. Discuss the likely effects of non-price competition for consumers. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)

Non-price competition means competing through branding, quality, innovation and service rather than price. Consumers may gain from rapid product development, such as better cameras and batteries, as firms try to differentiate. Wide choice and good after-sales service could improve satisfaction. Handset makers regularly release new models with new features.

However, heavy advertising and branding raise costs, which may be passed on as higher prices. Firms may also differentiate in minor ways that add little real value, and loyalty to a brand can allow firms to charge prices above marginal cost. High costs of development can create barriers to entry for smaller firms, reducing competition.

The net effect depends on how much consumers value the innovation compared with the price premium, and on whether competition between brands is intense enough to keep prices in check. On balance consumers are likely to gain where innovation is genuine and rivals are close competitors.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Oligopolists always act against the interests of consumers." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanTwo sides, real firms, and a judgement based on contestability and regulation.
  1. Define oligopoly and concentration. Link to efficiency and consumer surplus.
  2. Against consumers: collusion and price fixing, higher prices, limit and predatory pricing, barriers to entry, X-inefficiency, spending on advertising.
  3. For consumers: price wars, non-price competition, innovation funded by profit, economies of scale that lower costs, quality and choice.
  4. Constraints: competition authorities, fines for cartels, the threat of entry (contestability), buyer power of large retailers.
  5. Judgement: "always" is too strong. The outcome depends on whether firms collude, how contestable the market is and how effective regulators are.

Top tips

  • Concentration ratio: add the n biggest shares. Show the working.
  • Interdependence is the key idea. Firms think about rivals' reactions before acting.
  • Collusion: say whether it is overt or tacit, and name the incentive to cheat.
  • Prisoner's dilemma: describe the payoffs, name the dominant strategy, explain why both end up worse off.
  • Price versus non-price: use examples from the case study (branding, loyalty, innovation).
  • Evaluate with contestability, regulation, time period, and whether a high concentration ratio really means weak competition.
  • Do not draw a kinked demand curve unless the question asks: it is not required for this specification.
