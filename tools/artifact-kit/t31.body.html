Business Growth

Why some firms stay small and others grow, who really controls a firm, the four routes to growth, what holds growth back, and why firms sometimes split up again.

The big idea

A firm's size is a choice with costs and benefits. Growth can lower costs and raise market power, but it needs finance, brings risk, and can make the firm harder to run. Who decides, and for whose benefit, is the principal-agent problem. Spec 3.1.1 to 3.1.3.

3.1.1 Sizes and types of firm

Why some firms stay small

  • Niche or local market: demand is too small to support a larger firm (a village bakery, a hairdresser).
  • Owner objectives: the owner may prefer control, a quiet life or a lifestyle business to maximum profit.
  • Limited finance: small firms find it hard to borrow, and retained profit is small.
  • Diseconomies of scale: beyond a point, a bigger firm may be harder to manage and costs per unit may rise.
  • Personal service: the product depends on the owner's skill or close customer contact.
  • Risk and regulation: growth exposes owners to more risk and more rules.

Why others grow

  • Economies of scale that lower unit costs.
  • Market power: a bigger share lets the firm influence price and bargain with suppliers.
  • Profit and survival: larger firms may be better able to survive downturns and fund investment.
  • Risk spreading by selling in several markets or products.
  • Managers' and shareholders' aims such as status, pay linked to size, or higher share prices.
  • Barrier to rivals: scale can deter entry.

Divorce of ownership from control: the principal-agent problem

In a large company the owners (shareholders) are the principals and the managers they hire are the agents. Shareholders usually want profit and a rising share price. Managers may prefer higher pay, status, job security and a larger firm, which can mean revenue or sales maximisation instead of profit maximisation. Managers also know far more about the firm than shareholders do (asymmetric information), so they are hard to monitor.

Ways to narrow the gapLink pay to profit or share price (bonuses, share options), appoint non-executive directors, require audited accounts and annual shareholder votes, and use takeover threats: a badly run firm with a low share price is easier to buy.

Public and private sector

Private sector: owned by individuals or shareholders, run by private decisions (sole traders, partnerships, companies). Public sector: owned and controlled by the government (central or local), such as state schools or a council leisure centre. Public sector bodies are often set targets other than profit.

Profit and not-for-profit

Profit-making organisations aim to make a surplus for owners. Not-for-profit organisations (charities, social enterprises, some co-operatives) reinvest any surplus in their cause. They are not the same as the public sector: a charity is private-sector but not-for-profit.

3.1.2 How firms grow

MethodWhat it isExample
Organic (internal)Growth from the firm's own resources: new products, new outlets, more salesA bakery opening a fifth shop from retained profit
Forward vertical integrationJoins with a firm nearer the consumer in the supply chainA brewer buying pubs
Backward vertical integrationJoins with a firm nearer the raw materials (a supplier)A bakery chain buying a flour mill
Horizontal integrationJoins with a firm at the same stage of production in the same industryTwo supermarket chains merging
Conglomerate integrationJoins with a firm in an unrelated industryA food company buying a software firm

A merger is a mutual agreement to combine. A takeover (acquisition) is one firm buying control of another, sometimes against its wishes. Both are external growth.

Constraints on growth

Size of the market

If demand is small or already saturated, expansion only drives down price. Niche products have a natural ceiling.

Access to finance

Growth costs money. Retained profit is limited; banks may lend only against security; share issues may dilute control. External growth may need large funds.

Owner objectives

An owner who wants control or a stable lifestyle may refuse to expand, sell shares or take on debt.

Regulation

Competition authorities such as the CMA can investigate or block mergers that may reduce competition. Planning rules and sector regulation can also limit expansion.

3.1.3 Demergers

A demerger splits a firm into two or more separate businesses, for example by selling off a division or spinning it off as a new company with its own shareholders.

Reasons

  • Diseconomies of scale or a firm that has become too complex
  • Refocus on the core business
  • The parts may be worth more separately than the whole
  • Raise cash by selling a division
  • Competition authority may force it after a merger
  • A merger that failed, perhaps through culture clash
  • Pressure from shareholders

Impact on

Businesses: clearer focus, lower unit costs if diseconomies disappear, but lost economies of scale, less diversification and one-off costs.

Workers: redundancies and uncertainty are likely, though smaller firms may offer better promotion and a closer culture.

Consumers: could gain from more specialised products and more competition, or lose if scale benefits are lost and prices rise.

Do not mix upVertical integration is about the supply chain (backward or forward). Horizontal is the same stage. Conglomerate is unrelated. And "organic" is not a type of merger: it is growth without one.

5 · Multiple choice

Descriptions are kept general and based on widely reported events. Check current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Banking bonuses and the 2008 crisis

Before 2008, many bank managers were paid bonuses linked to short-term profit and trading volumes. Critics argued this encouraged risk-taking whose costs later fell on shareholders and taxpayers. Since then, bonuses have often been deferred and can be reclaimed (clawback).

Exam use: A clear principal-agent example: managers (agents) pursued pay linked to short-term profit, shareholders (principals) bore the risk. Solutions: long-term incentives, deferral, clawback.

Disney and 21st Century Fox

In 2019 Disney completed the purchase of most of 21st Century Fox's film and television businesses, in a deal reported at around $71 billion. Both firms produced and owned entertainment content. Regulators approved it, in some places with conditions such as selling certain assets.

Exam use: Horizontal integration: same stage, same industry. Link to economies of scale, market power over distributors, and regulatory conditions.

Microsoft and Activision

Microsoft's purchase of the video-game publisher Activision Blizzard was announced in 2022 at around $69 billion. The UK competition authority first blocked it in 2023, then cleared a restructured deal after Microsoft agreed to sell certain cloud-gaming rights. The deal completed in late 2023.

Exam use: Regulation as a constraint on growth, and benefits of mergers (access to games, scale). Compare with the 2025 Paper 1 question on Microsoft.

Meta and Giphy

Meta (then Facebook) bought the animated-image library Giphy in 2020. The UK competition authority concluded the deal could reduce competition and ordered Meta to sell it. Giphy was later sold to another company.

Exam use: A merger that harms competition can be unwound. Useful evaluation point on regulation and on mergers bought to remove potential rivals.

Demergers: GSK and Unilever

GSK split off its consumer health business as a separate company, Haleon, in 2022. Unilever announced in 2024 that it would separate its ice cream business, with the new company listed separately by the end of 2025.

Exam use: Reasons for demergers: refocus on the core business, the parts worth more apart, different growth and investment needs.

Organic growth: a high street baker

Some food retailers have grown mainly by opening more shops funded from their own profits, rather than by buying rivals. This is slower than a takeover but keeps control and avoids merger integration costs.

Exam use: Organic versus external growth: lower risk and less debt, but slower and limited by retained profit and the size of the market.

Exam-style questions

Written in the style of Edexcel Paper 1 and Paper 3. Open each question for reminders, a model answer and common mistakes. Answers are colour-coded to the mark scheme. These are practice questions, not past-paper wording.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain one reason why a firm may remain small.
ReminderOne reason only, developed. Name it, say why it limits size, and tie it to a type of business.
One reason is a niche or local market with limited demand. A village bakery, for example, may serve only a few hundred local customers. If the market is small, opening further shops could mean sales are spread thinly across too few customers, so extra revenue may not cover the extra costs of rent and wages. The owner may therefore judge that staying small gives a higher profit per unit of effort than expanding.
Common mistakeGiving two reasons in a "one reason" question, or writing "they do not have enough money" without explaining why that stops growth.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by the principal-agent problem in a large company.
ReminderSay who the principals and agents are, what each wants, and why the gap exists.
In a large company the shareholders (principals) own the firm but the managers (agents) run it day to day. Shareholders usually want profit, while managers may pursue their own aims such as higher pay or status. Because managers know more about the firm than shareholders (asymmetric information), they may be able to pursue sales or revenue maximisation, which could be at the expense of profit. For example, a manager paid on turnover could prefer a risky takeover that makes the firm larger even if profit per share falls.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks A brewery buys a chain of pubs. Analyse one benefit and one drawback of this form of integration.
ReminderName the type (forward vertical). One benefit with a chain, one drawback with a chain. No judgement needed.

This is forward vertical integration, because the brewer moves nearer the consumer in the supply chain. A benefit is that the brewery secures a guaranteed outlet for its beer, so demand is more stable and it keeps the retailer's profit margin, which could raise its total profit.

A drawback is that running pubs needs different skills from brewing, so management costs may rise and the pubs could perform badly. Rival brewers may also stop supplying the pubs or refuse to deal with the firm, so pubs sell a narrower range and customers may go elsewhere.

Q4 Assess 10 marks A food manufacturer is considering buying a smaller rival. Assess whether horizontal integration is likely to benefit the manufacturer. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)
ReminderTwo developed advantages, then evaluate with a counter-argument and a judgement. Mention the competition authority and culture clash.

Horizontal integration joins two firms at the same stage of production. The manufacturer could gain economies of scale, for example buying ingredients in bulk and spreading advertising and distribution costs over more units, which may lower long-run average cost and raise profit per unit. It would also remove a rival and increase its market share, so it could have more power to set price and bargain with supermarkets.

However, merging two firms may bring clashing cultures, duplicated roles and job losses, so diseconomies of scale could appear and costs might not fall as expected. The competition authority may also investigate the deal or demand disposals, and integration costs can be large. Overall the benefit is likely to depend on how similar the firms are and how big the cost savings are relative to the price paid. If the rival's products are complementary and the firms are well matched, the merger is more likely to succeed.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss the likely effects of a demerger on a large conglomerate's shareholders, workers and consumers. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)
ReminderCover all three groups with a short chain each, then judge who is most affected and what it depends on.

A demerger splits a firm into separate businesses. For shareholders, the parts may be valued more highly separately than together (a conglomerate discount), so the combined share value could rise, and each firm can focus on its core business. Shareholders may also be able to choose which business to hold, rather than being forced into a mix.

Workers may face redundancies as head-office roles are duplicated, and uncertainty about pay and conditions. A smaller company could, though, offer faster promotion and closer management. Consumers could gain from more specialised products and clearer service, and from more competition between the new firms. If lost economies of scale raise costs, prices could rise.

The impact is likely to be largest for workers in the divisions sold off, because job security is most at risk, while shareholders and consumers are likely to be affected gradually. Whether it benefits shareholders depends on the price obtained, the one-off costs of separation, and whether the business units really can operate alone. The effect also depends on whether the original merger created genuine cost savings.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Businesses are better to grow organically than by merging with or taking over other firms." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
Plan, then writeAim for four paragraphs of analysis (two each side) and a conclusion. Use a context: pick a real or imagined firm and keep returning to it.

Plan

  1. Define organic and external growth (merger/takeover; horizontal, vertical, conglomerate).
  2. For organic: lower risk, keeps control and culture, avoids integration costs, funded by retained profit.
  3. For external: faster, immediate market share and economies of scale, new markets, removes a rival.
  4. Against organic: slow, limited by finance and market size. Against mergers: culture clash, diseconomies, high price, regulatory block.
  5. Judgement: depends on the firm's size, objective, finance, market, and the type of integration. Say which factor you think is decisive and why.

Sample analysis paragraph

Organic growth means expanding using the firm's own resources. For a restaurant chain, opening a new branch funded by retained profit means the firm keeps control of its brand and does not take on large debt, so if the new site performs poorly the loss is limited. Because the firm grows gradually, managers can learn from each opening and keep the same culture and quality, which may protect the brand and customer loyalty. This lower risk could be especially valuable where owners have a lifestyle objective or where interest rates are high, since borrowing to finance a takeover would be costly.

Sample evaluation paragraph

However, organic growth is slow, so a rival who merges may gain economies of scale and market power first, leaving the organic firm unable to compete on price. Where a market is growing fast or consolidating, speed may matter more than risk. It also depends on the type of merger: a vertical merger that secures supplies may be more valuable than a conglomerate one that adds little.

Common mistakeListing advantages of mergers with no organic comparison. The question asks which is better, so every paragraph should compare.

Top tips

  • Name the type of integration exactly. Forward, backward, horizontal or conglomerate, and say why: "nearer the consumer", "same stage", "unrelated".
  • Use the context. Application marks need the firm or extract: its products, markets, size or objectives.
  • Chain every point. Merger → bulk buying → lower average cost → price cuts or higher margin.
  • Principal-agent: name both parties, say what each wants, and why the gap matters (asymmetric information).
  • Do not call every merger good. Evaluation is about culture clash, diseconomies, finance and regulation.
  • Evaluate with "depends on". Size of firm, type of market, finance available, owner objectives, time period.
  • Demergers: give reasons first, then impacts on at least two groups.
