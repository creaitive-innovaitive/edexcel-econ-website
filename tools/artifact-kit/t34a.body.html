Efficiency, Perfect Competition and Monopolistic Competition

Four kinds of efficiency, then two competitive market structures: why perfect competition reaches the efficient benchmark and why monopolistic competition falls just short.

The big idea

Market structure describes how competitive a market is: how many firms, how similar their products, and how easy it is to enter. It decides how much power a firm has over price and whether its profit lasts. Efficiency is the yardstick we use to judge the result.

3.4.1 Efficiency

Allocative efficiency

Resources are allocated to produce the goods consumers most want. Reached where P = MC: the price consumers will pay for the last unit equals the cost of resources used to make it.

Productive efficiency

Output is produced at the lowest possible average cost: at the bottom of the AC curve, where MC = AC. No resources are wasted for the output produced.

Dynamic efficiency

Efficiency over time: investment, research and innovation that lower costs and improve products. Funded by profit, so firms with some supernormal profit may be better placed.

X-inefficiency

Costs are higher than they need to be for a given output: the firm operates above its AC curve. Likely when a firm faces little competitive pressure, so managers allow slack, waste and high pay.

Link to Theme 1Allocative efficiency is the same idea as the point where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost. In this chapter we use P = MC, which assumes no externalities.

3.4.2 Perfect competition

Characteristics

  • Many buyers and many sellers, each too small to affect price: firms are price takers
  • Homogeneous (identical) product
  • Perfect information about prices and quality
  • No barriers to entry or exit; factors of production are mobile
  • Firms are profit maximisers

Revenue

Demand for one firm's output is perfectly elastic at the market price, so AR = MR = P: a horizontal line. Selling one more unit adds exactly the market price.

Realistic examples are rare. Agricultural markets and some foreign-exchange markets come close.

Short run and long run

  • Short run: the firm produces where MC = MR. If price is above AC it earns supernormal profit; if below, it makes a loss (and shuts down if price falls below AVC).
  • Supernormal profit attracts new firms, so market supply shifts right, price falls and profit is competed away.
  • Losses make firms leave, supply shifts left, price rises.
  • Long run: only normal profit remains: AR = AC. Because the firm is at the bottom of its AC curve, P = MC = AC: both allocative and productive efficiency.
Evaluation pointPerfect competition may not be dynamically efficient: with only normal profit in the long run there is little surplus to fund R&D. Homogeneous products also give consumers no variety.

3.4.3 Monopolistic competition

Characteristics

  • Many firms
  • Differentiated products (branding, quality, location, service)
  • Low barriers to entry and exit
  • Some price-making power, so the demand curve slopes down
  • Heavy use of non-price competition
  • Examples: restaurants, hairdressers, independent bakeries

Equilibrium

Short run: produce where MC = MR, read price off AR. If AR is above AC the firm earns supernormal profit.

Long run: entry shifts each firm's demand curve left (and makes it more elastic) until AR touches AC at one point: normal profit, with AR tangent to AC.

Why it falls short of efficiencyIn the long run the tangency is on the falling part of AC. So price is above MC (not allocatively efficient) and output is below the minimum AC (not productively efficient). The firm has excess capacity. Consumers do get variety, and firms may innovate to differentiate, so dynamic efficiency is possible.

Exam-style questions

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by allocative efficiency.
ReminderState the condition (P = MC) and say why it matters for consumers and resources.
Allocative efficiency occurs when resources are allocated so that the goods and services produced are those consumers most want. It is reached where price equals marginal cost (P = MC). At this point the value consumers place on the last unit, shown by the price they are willing to pay, equals the opportunity cost of the resources used to produce it. If price were above MC, society would gain from producing more; if below, resources would be better used elsewhere.
Q2 Calculate 2 marks A firm in perfect competition sells at £8 a unit and produces 100 units. Its average cost is £6.50. Calculate its total profit.
ReminderProfit = (AR − AC) × Q, or TR − TC.
TR = 8 × 100 = £800; TC = 6.50 × 100 = £650. Profit = 800 − 650 = £150 (supernormal profit).
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse why a perfectly competitive firm that is making supernormal profit in the short run will earn only normal profit in the long run.
ReminderChain: supernormal profit → entry (no barriers) → supply shifts right → price falls → profit eroded. Use a diagram.

In perfect competition there are no barriers to entry and firms have perfect information, so they can see that existing firms are making supernormal profit. New firms enter the industry, so the market supply curve shifts to the right. With demand unchanged, the equilibrium market price falls, so the horizontal AR = MR curve of each firm moves down. The firm still produces where MC = MR, but as price falls the gap between AR and AC narrows. Entry continues until price equals the minimum of AC, where AR = AC. At this point only normal profit is earned, so there is no incentive for further entry.

Common mistakeForgetting to say the market supply curve shifts, not the firm's. The firm's own AR line falls because the market price falls.
Q4 Assess 10 marks Assess whether a perfectly competitive market is more efficient than a monopolistically competitive one. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)
ReminderTwo types of efficiency analysed, then evaluate with variety and dynamic efficiency.

In the long run a perfectly competitive firm produces where P = MC and at the minimum point of AC, so it is both allocatively and productively efficient. A monopolistically competitive firm faces a downward-sloping AR curve, so MC = MR gives a price above MC, which means resources are under-allocated to the product. Entry leaves AR tangent to AC on the falling part of the curve, so output is below the minimum-cost level and the firm has excess capacity, meaning higher average costs.

However, perfect competition requires identical products, so consumers have no choice, whereas monopolistic competition delivers variety, which many consumers value. Firms in monopolistic competition may also invest in innovation to differentiate, giving some dynamic efficiency, while perfectly competitive firms earn only normal profit and may have little to invest. Overall, perfect competition is more efficient in a static sense, but it is an ideal that few markets match, so the comparison is limited by how far real markets satisfy the assumptions.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Many independent coffee shops in a city centre compete with each other. Discuss the likely effects on consumers of this market structure. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)
ReminderSpot the structure (monopolistic competition) and use it: differentiation, low barriers, normal profit in the long run.

The coffee shops resemble monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products and low barriers to entry. Consumers are likely to benefit from wide choice of blends, atmosphere and location, and firms compete on quality and service to keep customers. Because entry is easy, if existing shops make supernormal profit new shops are likely to open, which could keep prices lower than if there were few shops and could stop profit rising in the long run.

Each shop has some price-setting power because customers value its brand, so price may be above marginal cost, and with excess capacity average costs are higher than the minimum, which could mean consumers pay more than in perfect competition. Spending on branding and advertising may raise costs too.

The overall effect depends on how much consumers value variety and how easily they can compare prices. In a city centre, high rents could limit entry, meaning shops keep supernormal profit for longer and the benefit to consumers is smaller. On balance, consumers are likely to gain more from choice and competition than they lose from slightly higher prices.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Competition between many small firms always benefits consumers." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanMake the chains explicit and judge on conditions, not on "yes" or "no".
  1. Define the market structures involved (perfect and monopolistic competition) and the efficiency tests.
  2. For: low prices (P close to MC), normal profit only in the long run, entry removes supernormal profit, productive and allocative efficiency in perfect competition, choice in monopolistic competition.
  3. Against: little scope for economies of scale (small firms have higher average costs), little profit for R&D, excess capacity, spending on branding, limited quality control.
  4. Compare with concentrated markets where economies of scale could lower costs, and large firms invest more.
  5. Judgement: depends on the type of good (homogeneous or differentiated), the size of economies of scale and how contestable the market is.

Top tips

  • Draw the diagram. Firm diagram with AR, MR, MC, AC. Mark Q*, price, AC at Q* and shade profit.
  • AR = MR for a price taker. A horizontal line. Monopolistic competition has a downward-sloping AR and MR below it.
  • MC cuts AC at its lowest point. If your MC does not, you lose the mark.
  • Long-run perfect competition: P = MC = min AC.
  • Long-run monopolistic competition: AR is tangent to AC, so P > MC and output is below the minimum-cost output.
  • Name the efficiency. Productive is minimum AC. Allocative is P = MC. Dynamic is over time. X-inefficiency is slack costs.
  • Evaluate with the real world. Perfect competition is a benchmark; few markets match it.
