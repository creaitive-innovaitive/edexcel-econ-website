Government Intervention: Competition and Regulation

How governments control mergers and monopoly power, promote competition, and protect suppliers and workers, what each policy does to price, profit, efficiency, quality and choice, and why regulators can get it wrong.

The big idea

Markets with market power can charge too much, produce too little and become inefficient. Governments can control the growth of that power (merger control), regulate it (price, profit, quality, performance), make markets more competitive (small business support, deregulation, tendering, privatisation) and protect the weaker side (restricting monopsony, nationalisation). Each policy has costs, and regulators themselves can fail.

3.6.1 Forms of intervention

a) Controlling mergers

In the UK the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) investigates mergers that could lead to a substantial lessening of competition, for example where the combined firm would have around a quarter of the market. It can allow the merger, block it, or allow it with remedies such as selling off some businesses.

Benefits

Prevents firms gaining excessive market power, protects consumer choice and keeps pressure on firms to be efficient.

Drawbacks

May stop mergers that would create economies of scale and lower prices; investigations are costly and slow; firms may avoid scrutiny by growing differently.

d) Protecting suppliers and employees

  • Restrictions on monopsony power: e.g. a groceries code that protects suppliers from unfair terms and late payment; minimum wages; competition rules on buyer power. They aim to raise the price suppliers or workers receive and protect firms from being squeezed out.
  • Nationalisation: government takes ownership of a private firm. Benefits: pursue social aims (jobs, access), prevent exploitation by a private monopoly, protect strategic industries. Drawbacks: possible X-inefficiency, taxpayer cost, political interference, no profit incentive.

3.6.2 Impact and limits

a) Impact on prices, profit, efficiency, quality and choice

Use the Explore tab to compare each policy on those five outcomes. As a rule: policies that increase competition tend to lower prices and profits and raise choice and efficiency; policies that regulate a monopoly aim to lower prices and profits but risk lower quality or investment; nationalisation and privatisation change the objective of the firm.

b) Limits to government intervention

Regulatory capture

The regulator comes to act in the interests of the industry it regulates, rather than consumers. Causes: reliance on the firms for information, personal links ("revolving door" of staff between regulator and firms), lobbying and sharing of expertise. Result: weak caps, light enforcement.

Asymmetric information

The firm knows more about its true costs and demand than the regulator. The regulator may set a price cap too high (letting the firm keep excess profit) or too low (threatening investment and quality). It also makes targets easy to game.

Evaluation pointRegulation has costs of its own (administration, compliance) and may be government failure. The best policy depends on the market: a natural monopoly usually needs regulation; a market with many potential entrants may need only lower barriers to entry.

5 · Multiple choice

Descriptions are kept general and based on widely reported events. Check current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Blocking a supermarket merger

In 2019 the UK competition authority blocked the proposed merger of Sainsbury's and Asda, concluding that it could reduce competition and raise prices for shoppers.

Exam use: Merger control in action. Benefits (protects competition) versus drawbacks (the firms argued for lower prices through scale).

Regulating water

Ofwat sets five-year price limits for water companies. Financial strain and sewage discharge failures at some companies, notably Thames Water, have led to calls for tougher regulation.

Exam use: Price regulation of a natural monopoly and its limits: debt, under-investment and asymmetric information.

The energy price cap

Since 2019 Ofgem has capped what suppliers can charge households on default tariffs, adjusting it regularly. The cap rose sharply in 2022 as wholesale gas prices jumped, and the government added further support.

Exam use: Price regulation: the aim of protecting consumers, the risk to supplier finances, and why caps follow costs. Links to a recent Paper 1 essay.

Railway ownership

British Rail was privatised in the 1990s, and the track was later put under Network Rail. Since 2018 several train operators have returned to public ownership, and more have followed.

Exam use: Privatisation and nationalisation: efficiency and investment versus social aims and cost to taxpayers.

Carillion and public contracts

Carillion, a major government contractor, collapsed in 2018. Critics argued competitive tendering and low bids had left the firm financially fragile and exposed public services.

Exam use: A drawback of competitive tendering: low bids and cost cutting can risk quality and failure.

Regulatory capture and aviation

After two Boeing 737 MAX crashes in 2018 and 2019, critics argued that the US safety regulator relied too heavily on the manufacturer to certify its own work.

Exam use: Regulatory capture and asymmetric information: the regulator depends on the firm it regulates.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain how a price cap (CPI − X) regulation might affect a regulated monopoly.
A price cap limits how much a regulated firm can raise its prices each year, typically to the rate of inflation (CPI) minus an efficiency factor X. If the firm cuts costs by more than X it keeps the extra as profit, so it has an incentive to be more efficient. For example, a water company allowed to raise bills by inflation minus 1% must find efficiencies to protect its profit. But if the cap is too tight, the firm may cut quality or investment, so consumers may not benefit.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by regulatory capture.
Regulatory capture occurs when a regulator comes to act in the interests of the industry it is supposed to regulate rather than in the interests of consumers. This can happen because the regulator relies on the firms for information, because staff move between the regulator and the industry, or because firms lobby effectively. For instance, an energy regulator might allow higher price caps than costs justify after persuasive lobbying. As a result, firms keep supernormal profit and consumers pay more than they should.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks A local council introduces competitive tendering for its refuse collection. Analyse the likely effects on costs and service quality.
ReminderOne chain for costs, one for quality. Say what could go right and what could go wrong.

Competitive tendering means private firms bid for the contract to provide the service. Because firms compete to win, they have an incentive to submit lower bids and to reduce costs through efficiency and technology, so the council is likely to pay less and could free funds for other spending.

To keep profit at a low contract price, firms may cut staff, pay or equipment standards, which could lower service quality, for example missed collections. The council also faces the cost of writing contracts and monitoring performance. Quality standards and penalty clauses could reduce this risk.

Q4 Assess 10 marks Assess whether privatisation of a state-owned rail operator is likely to benefit passengers. (KAA 6 + evaluation 4)

Privatisation transfers ownership from the public to the private sector, introducing a profit motive. Private owners have an incentive to cut costs and may invest in new trains or better services, improving punctuality and comfort, and competition between train companies could lower fares. The government also gains sale revenue and reduced subsidy.

However, rail is close to a natural monopoly on many routes, so a private firm could exploit passengers with high fares unless regulated, and may cut unprofitable services. Profit may be put before service quality, and regulation is costly and open to regulatory capture. Overall, passengers are more likely to benefit where there is real competition or strong regulation of fares and quality. Without these, the benefits of the profit motive may go to shareholders instead.

Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss the likely effects of the government blocking a merger between two large supermarkets. (KAA 8 + evaluation 4)

The competition authority may block a merger that is likely to substantially lessen competition. Blocking it keeps more rival supermarkets in the market, so consumers are likely to retain choice and competitive pressure on prices and quality. It also prevents the merged firm from gaining more bargaining power over suppliers (monopsony power), protecting farmers and manufacturers. Supermarkets might have to continue competing on price and quality to keep shoppers.

But blocking may stop economies of scale such as bulk-buying, which could have lowered costs and prices. Firms that had planned to merge could suffer lower profit, and the investigation is costly.

The net effect depends on how large the likely economies of scale are compared with the loss of competition, and on how contestable the market is. If entry by discounters is easy, the competition loss from the merger may be small and blocking could lower benefits. The authority may also lack full information, so its decision may be wrong.

Q6 Evaluate 25 marks "Government intervention to regulate monopoly power does more harm than good." Evaluate this view. (KAA 16 + evaluation 9)
PlanShow the market failure first. Then at least two policies analysed with chains, then government failure.
  1. Market failure from monopoly: P > MC, deadweight loss, X-inefficiency; define the policies (price caps, profit regulation, merger control, deregulation, privatisation).
  2. Benefits: price caps cut prices and incentivise cost cutting; merger control protects competition; deregulation lowers barriers; competitive tendering cuts costs.
  3. Harms: regulatory capture, asymmetric information, quality cuts, under-investment, cost of regulation, loss of scale economies.
  4. Compare with leaving the market alone: contestability might discipline firms; natural monopolies still need regulation.
  5. Judgement: depends on the market type, regulator quality and the policy. "Does more harm than good" is too strong for natural monopolies with clear price abuse.

Top tips

  • Name the policy exactly (price cap, rate-of-return regulation, merger control, competitive tendering) and say how it works.
  • Use the five outcomes: price, profit, efficiency, quality, choice.
  • Price caps: mention CPI − X and the incentive to cut costs, and the danger to quality.
  • Limits: regulatory capture and asymmetric information appear in nearly every evaluation.
  • Evaluate with the market: natural monopoly, contestability, size of economies of scale, time period.
  • Do not say regulation is "always bad". Compare it with doing nothing.
  • Use UK bodies as examples: CMA, Ofwat, Ofgem, Ofcom, Office of Rail and Road.
